# Sessions & Notes

The session ends, everyone goes home, and within a week half the table has forgotten the NPC's name, the other half misremembers the deal, and the GM's notebook says something illegible about "the lantern??" Sessions and notes exist so that the campaign remembers what actually happened — who was there, what each person saw, and what it means next time.

The unit of organization is the session: a dated point on the campaign timeline with a roster, a schedule, and everyone's notes attached. Each participant writes their own account — the rogue's player remembers the lockpicking, the GM remembers what was behind the door — and Esiana also composes the combined view: all authors side by side, entities mentioned, references aggregated. Nobody has to reconcile five notebooks into one truth, because the system keeps every perspective and shows them together.

## How it works

Everything hangs off the session timeline, an ordered list of session points. Creating a session anchors it to a date; scheduling it proposes a real-world time and venue, publishes it to the party, and collects RSVPs. Attendance tracks who was actually there, which matters more than it sounds — "who was present when the deal was struck" resolves real table arguments.

Notes come in two layers. Personal notes are each author's own write-up for a session, including GM-only notes other players never see. The combined view lays every author's notes for a session side by side with a ribbon of mentioned entities and references, so the table shares one page that still preserves every viewpoint. Compiling turns a session or range of sessions into a Markdown recap — the artifact you send to the player who missed the game, or archive at arc's end.

Notebook arcs cut across the timeline for material that is not one session's story: a character's journal, an investigation file, a faction dossier compiled over months. Pages file into arcs regardless of which session spawned them, and arcs can be renamed, reorganized, and dissolved without touching the sessions underneath.

Two practical comforts round it out. Notes can be imported from plain text, Markdown, or Word documents, landing as session notes authored by the uploader. And players get a private sandbox — scratch space nobody else sees — for schemes, theories-to-be, and drafts not ready for the party view.

Scheduling has its own small loop. The GM drafts a schedule (date, time, venue, linked location), publishes it, and the party RSVPs. Changes and cancellations notify everyone; a reminder goes out before the game. An RSVP digest keeps the GM informed without a chatter of individual pings.

## Using sessions and notes

A normal session cycle looks like this:

1. The GM creates the session point and publishes the schedule. Players RSVP from the session page or their Campaign Home.
2. You play. Attendance gets marked — who was actually at the table.
3. Afterward, everyone writes their own notes in their own column: what happened, what their character noticed, what they suspect.
4. Anyone catching up opens the combined view: every perspective at once, entities and references aggregated, GM-only material correctly absent for players.
5. When a recap is needed, compile the session (or the arc) to Markdown and share or archive it.

For ongoing organization, file long-running material into notebook arcs as it accumulates — the investigation deserves its own arc from the start, not a rescue operation six sessions in. And when a player misses a game, point them at the combined view first and the compiled recap second; both exist so absence does not become exile.

Uploading works where you would expect: from the session notes area, import a text, Markdown, or Word file and it arrives as your authored note, wikilinks and all.

## Campaign configuration

Session management is a GM and Co-DM surface: creating sessions, publishing schedules, and organizing notebooks. Players write their own notes and RSVP; they do not rearrange the timeline. Compile size caps and upload limits may be bounded by the instance administrator, which surfaces as limits on very large exports rather than campaign settings. Notification behavior for scheduling (publish, change, cancel, reminders) follows each user's notification preferences, with email delivery depending on the instance having mail configured.

## Players

This feature is substantially yours. Your notes column is your voice in the campaign record — write what your character saw, not the objective truth, because the combined view preserves the difference. Your sandbox is private; your session notes are visible per the campaign's sharing rules, with GM-only material withheld. RSVP promptly; the GM's digest, the headcount for the venue, and the reminder logic all run off it. And when you miss a session, the combined view plus the compiled recap are the catch-up path — read them before asking the table to re-play the game for you.

## Things to know

GM-only notes stay GM-only in every surface, including combined views, compiles, and exports — but only if they were written as GM notes in the first place. A secret typed into a party-visible column is party-visible; the system cannot tell you meant it privately. Compiled recaps inherit the visibility of what went into them, so think before forwarding a compile that mixed GM material into the batch. Attendance is worth marking accurately: "since your last visit" summaries, RSVP digests, and several recap behaviors key off who was actually there.
