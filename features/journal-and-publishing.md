# Journal & Publishing

Every campaign produces writing that deserves better than a chat log: the in-world broadsheet the party reads aloud, the captain's log, the obituary for the paladin, the proclamation nailed to the city gates. The journal is Esiana's small press — a place to write that material, shape it through drafts and review, and publish it to the table on purpose instead of leaking it by accident.

The distinction that organizes everything here is draft versus released. A draft is private working material: unfinished, unscheduled, invisible to anyone who should not see it yet. A released issue is table canon, listed in the library, readable by its audience. Everything between — series, rules, evaluation — exists to move writing safely from the first state to the second.

## How it works

Publications are the individual pieces: a newsletter, letter, notice, dispatch, obituary, rumor sheet, journal entry, or proclamation. Each has a title, body text, tags, and a status that moves one direction through draft, scheduled, released, with archived as the dignified end. Content readiness is a real check, not a suggestion — an issue needs an actual title and actual body before it can release, and the system refuses to publish emptiness even when overridden in haste.

Series turn one-offs into institutions. A series — "The Winterfort Crier," "Ship's Log of the Drowning Lantern" — defines the recurring publication: its default form, its naming, and optionally the rule for minting the next issue. Generating the next issue drafts it automatically, numbered and attached to the series, ready to write. The planner view shows the whole queue at once: drafts and scheduled issues with their readiness, their rules, and what still blocks each one, plus recent releases for context.

Release rules are the most powerful and least obvious piece. A rule says *when this issue may go out* as a small logic tree over the campaign: after session twelve, once the in-world date passes Frostfall, when the war-thread resolves, when the project completes, when the page is revealed to the party — combined with all/any logic, plus manual gates a human must open. Evaluation previews the verdict without changing anything: ready, blocked, what is unmet. Release itself can be manual, automatic when conditions clear, or an explicit override — and even overrides require real content, because some lines should not be crossable.

The library is where released issues live for readers: browsable by recency, type, series, tag, or search, newest first. The planner is the back room; the library is the newsstand.

## Using the journal

A working press runs a loop per issue:

1. Draft the piece: title, body, type, tags. It starts as a draft, visible only per its current permissions.
2. Attach it to a series if it belongs to one, or generate the next issue from the series to start numbered and filed correctly.
3. If timing matters, set a release rule — the in-world date, the session count, the quest state, the manual gate — instead of trusting memory to publish on the right day.
4. Evaluate before release day. The planner shows readiness, rule presence, and unmet conditions; fix what is red.
5. Release when ready — manually, on schedule, or by override — and find it in the library where the table actually reads.

A standing series runs itself once configured: generate the next issue after each session, write it during the week, let the rule hold it until the fiction catches up, and release into a library that accumulates into a genuine archive of the campaign's public voice. The obituary series nobody planned and everybody remembers starts exactly this way too.

## Campaign configuration

Journal authorship follows page-creation permission — whoever may write pages may draft. The planner, release rules, evaluation, and release itself require planner access, which in practice means GMs and designated writers; the table decides who holds it through roles and collaboration settings. Series creation is open to authors; changing a series' automation and generating issues sit behind planner access. Deleting a released issue is deliberately hard — archive instead, or bring the campaign owner and an explicit confirmation — because released writing is canon other pages may reference.

## Players

Players read the library: the broadsheets, letters, proclamations, and rumor sheets the campaign publishes. Scheduled but unreleased issues are invisible, including their bodies — you cannot peek at next week's Crier by guessing URLs. If your table grants you authorship, you can draft and propose; release authority still typically rests with the GM side, which is why your brilliant dispatch waits for approval like everyone else's.

## Things to know

Release is one-way in spirit: an issue can return to draft or archive, but anything the table already read cannot be unread — unpublishing removes the copy, not the memory. Rules evaluate against live campaign state, so an issue scheduled on "quest resolved" releases the moment the quest resolves, even mid-session; time your rules for the fiction you want. Content readiness gates even overrides, so "publish now" on an empty draft fails rather than publishing a blank page to the table. And the library is reads, not storage — the bodies live in the issues, so deleting a series detaches its issues rather than destroying them.
