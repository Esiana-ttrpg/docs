# Narrative Threads

Long campaigns run on open loops: the masked stranger's promise, the prophecy half-remembered, the locked door in session three that nobody has opened yet. GMs track these in scattered notes, forget half of them, and resolve the rest by accident. Threads give those loops a home — one page per unresolved arc, with a state that says where it stands and machinery that notices when it goes stale.

A thread is a wiki page with a job. Where a location page describes a place, a thread page tracks an arc: what was set up, what would pay it off, what the party currently believes, and whether it is still alive. Mysteries, promises, foreshadowing, clues, and player theories are all threads; the kind only changes which questions the page prompts you to answer.

## How it works

Every thread moves through a lifecycle: locked, discovered, active, completed or failed. The lifecycle doubles as a visibility rule. A locked thread is invisible to players — use it while you are still preparing the payoff. Discovered and active threads appear to the party, which is how a mystery surfaces in their view at the right moment. Completed and failed are endings, and both are worth recording: a failed thread ("the rebellion was crushed before it began") is still resolved fiction, not a deleted draft.

Within the lifecycle sits a simpler status — open, dormant, resolved, abandoned — for day-to-day triage. An open thread is live; a dormant one is deliberately parked ("the dragon sleeps; revisit in Act III"); resolved and abandoned are two flavors of done. The campaign's Living Threads widget surfaces the open authored threads plus recent resolutions, so the table shares one answer to "what are we in the middle of?"

Threads also watch themselves. Esiana flags structural risks for the GM: foreshadowing with no payoff page linked, promises untouched for many sessions, clues nobody followed up. These are heuristics, not judgments — a dangling thread can be a deliberate slow burn — but they turn "did I forget something?" from anxiety into a checklist.

Players participate through theories. A theory is a thread kind reserved for player speculation: non-canonical by definition, visible to the table, and never confused with the GM's authored setup. When the rogue posts "I think the innkeeper is the Gray Man," that theory lives alongside the mystery it guesses at, and resolving the mystery can resolve the theory with it.

## Using threads

The working loop is small. When you plant something — a prophecy, a debt, a locked door — create a thread from the Threads hub or from the entity page it concerns, pick its kind, and write the setup: what was promised, to whom, and what payoff would look like, linked to the relevant pages. Keep it locked while you prep. When the arc surfaces in play, advance it to discovered or active so the party sees it in their thread lists. Update it as events unfold — new clues appended, status flipped to dormant when it sleeps — and close it when it resolves, one way or the other.

Concretely:

1. Create the thread where it belongs: the Threads hub for campaign-level arcs, or directly from a character, location, or quest page for arcs anchored there.
2. Classify it (mystery, promise, foreshadowing, clue) and write the setup: the hook, the stakes, and a link to wherever payoff should land.
3. Leave it locked until the arc is actually in play, then advance the lifecycle so the party can see it.
4. As sessions unfold, append developments, link new evidence, and park slow arcs as dormant rather than deleting them.
5. Resolve honestly — completed or failed — and let the resolution show in the Living Threads widget.

Periodically open the threads hub and read the GM warnings like a pre-session checklist. A stale promise two sessions before its payoff date is a gift, not a scolding.

## Campaign configuration

Threads need no dedicated configuration. Who can create and advance them follows the campaign's roles and collaboration permissions — typically GMs and writers for authored threads, with players able to submit theories. Lifecycle transitions on quest- and thread-linked pages may additionally require elevated editing rights, so a writer who can edit prose may still need the GM to advance a lifecycle state.

## Players

Players see discovered and active threads (never locked ones) and can submit theories from the Threads hub. Your theory is visible to the table by design — it is speculation, not a secret note to the GM. If you want to scheme privately, use your own session notes instead. Resolved threads stay readable, which makes the hub a satisfying record of everything the table actually accomplished.

## Things to know

Lock discipline is the whole game: a thread advanced to active too early spoils its own mystery in the party's thread list, while a thread left locked after it surfaced in play confuses players who go looking for it. Theories are second-class in exactly one way — they never count as canon setup — so GMs should not run plots off theory pages. And diagnostics flag possibilities, not errors: an "unresolved promise" the GM intends to pay off in the finale should stay right where it is.
