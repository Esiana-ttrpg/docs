# World Advance

Between sessions, the world should keep living. While the party rested, did the Gray Cartel tighten its grip on the docks? Did the famine deepen? Did the war the party ignored finally end — and who won? Answering those questions by hand for every faction and region is the part of GM prep that eats evenings. World advance does the bookkeeping: you describe how much time passed and which parts of the world should move, preview exactly what would change, and then apply it to canon all at once.

The key distinction — worth stating plainly because it confuses everyone once — is between moving the clock and moving the world. Advancing the campaign date changes what day it is. Advancing the world simulates what factions, territories, economies, conflicts, seasons, and traveling NPCs *did* with the elapsed time. A travel montage wants the clock. The five quiet years between arcs want both.

## How it works

A world advance is a batch: a bundle of proposed changes plus an optional clock step, assembled, previewed, and applied as one unit. You choose the scope — how much time, which domains (faction intrigue, territorial control, economy, conflict, seasons, NPC movement), anchored to which places — and Esiana previews the batch grouped by domain: what each effect would do, what conditions it reads (a region's stability, its prosperity, the pressure it is under), and a plain-language synthesis of the causal story ("the blockade starved the lower city; unrest rises").

Nothing in the preview is canon yet. That is the point of the two-step design: the preview is a draft of history, and applying writes it. On apply, the batch moves the clock if you asked it to, writes each effect into the world (wiki updates, faction and territory shifts, economic deltas), schedules or fires linked consequences, and records the whole batch as a dated chronology entry under a World Advance category — so the timeline remembers not just that time passed, but what the world did with it. A batch history keeps every past advance inspectable.

What world advance deliberately does *not* do matters as much. It does not run an autonomous AI simulation, it does not redraw map geometry on its own, and it does not spread rumors by itself — rumors travel when someone spreads them. The system proposes deterministic, inspectable changes from the state you authored; the judgment calls remain yours, exercised at preview time.

## Using world advance

The rhythm follows downtime: the party rests, travels, or skips ahead, and you advance the world to match.

1. Open world advance from time tracking or chronology. Set the duration and the domains in play — a quiet winter needs seasons and economy; the aftermath of an ignored war needs factions, conflict, and territory.
2. Read the preview the way you would read a draft: per-domain effects, the conditions behind them, the narrative synthesis. If something feels wrong — the famine should not have ended — adjust scope or inputs and re-preview rather than fixing canon afterward.
3. Apply. The clock steps, the effects land, and the batch is recorded as a chronology entry you can revisit later.
4. Handle the fallout in fiction: update or reveal the pages the batch touched, spread the rumors the party would now hear, and let the next session open on a world that visibly moved.

Pre-authored scheduled effects — recurring hooks like seasonal upkeep or timed threats — are evaluated as part of an advance, which is why setting them up before a big skip pays off: the batch does the remembering for you.

## Campaign configuration

World advance is a GM operation governed by chronology-management permission: game masters always qualify, and whether writers or players can participate follows the campaign's collaboration settings. There are no world-advance-specific toggles beyond that and the per-advance scope choices (duration, domains, anchors). The pressure and momentum surfaces that inform advances have their own displays but no player-facing controls — they are instruments, not settings.

## Players

Players never touch world advance directly, and that is intentional: the world moving without their input is the experience. What players notice is the aftermath — changed faction standings, new rumors in the feed, a city under different management — delivered through the normal discovery and recap surfaces. If the world seems to have jumped while you were away, it did; ask in character what happened.

## Things to know

Preview is a draft and apply is canon, and there is no undo button for a batch — re-preview until the draft reads right, because applied effects land in wiki state, faction standings, and the chronology record together. Advances need a master calendar to step time against; without one, time-based effects cannot resolve. Rumors do not diffuse on their own, so a world-shaking advance with no corresponding rumors will feel strangely quiet to the party — spread the word as part of the fallout. And keep the clock/world distinction straight when delegating: a writer who may advance the date may not be permitted to advance the world.

## Related features

- Chronology and calendars, for the clock advances step
- Downtime and projects, for the havens and undertakings advances act on
- Campaign history and snapshots, for freezing the before-and-after
- Narrative threads, for arcs that resolve through pressure rather than scenes
