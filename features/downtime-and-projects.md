# Downtime & Projects

Adventuring is the part of the campaign everyone sees. Downtime is everything the world does while nobody is looking: the stronghold being built, the treasury rising and falling, the faction's influence spreading through the city, the three months of training that turn a squire into a knight. Esiana's downtime system tracks that off-screen life with the same seriousness as the on-screen kind — because campaigns are remembered as much for what changed between adventures as for the adventures themselves.

## How it works

Downtime organizes around four kinds of things, and each answers a different question.

Havens answer "where is safe?" A haven is a held place with character: a home base, a sanctuary, a guildhall, a hidden refuge. Each haven tracks its condition and character — how it is doing, how large, who owns it, what theme defines it, who lives there, what threats loom over it, what quiet benefits it confers. A haven also keeps an activity log, the diary of everything that happened to it while the party was away. Creating a haven makes both a wiki page (so it reads like lore) and a living record (so it behaves like a place).

Projects answer "what are we building?" A project is an undertaking with a beginning, an end, and a status that moves through a real progression: planned, active, paused, completed — or failed, abandoned, suspended, the honest endings undertakings sometimes have. Researching the ritual, constructing the watchtower, training the militia, recovering the stolen relic: each gets its type, priority, owner, duration, and linked haven. Illegal status jumps are refused — a planned project cannot be declared completed without passing through active — which sounds bureaucratic until the first time it stops a table from accidentally "finishing" the cathedral in week two.

The ledger answers "what can we afford?" It is the campaign treasury: credits and debits in a named currency, debts tracked separately from spending, categories keeping the books readable, every entry stamped with when it happened in world time. Players can contribute entries — the rogue logs the fencing proceeds — while only the GM and writers change the books' setup or rewrite history. The balance is always opening funds plus everything since, computed live rather than stored and left to drift.

Suggestions answer "what did the world propose?" Much of downtime arrives as proposals rather than facts: ledger entries the system generated from upkeep and income, reputation shifts the fiction implies, world events the simulation surfaces. These sit in queues — ledger suggestions, reputation suggestions, world-event suggestions — until someone with authority accepts them into canon or dismisses them. Accepting a world event can create a real calendar event; accepting a reputation shift records it against a faction. Until accepted, a suggestion changes nothing, which makes the queues safe to ignore and powerful to triage.

Behind the queues runs scheduled automation: recurring effects the GM defines once — weekly upkeep costs, monthly income, seasonal threat prompts — that fire as campaign time advances and land as new suggestions or prompts. And gap annotations let chroniclers note who was present, absent, or affected during stretches of the timeline the sessions skipped, so the record stays honest about the quiet periods.

## Using downtime

The shape of a downtime session — the fifteen minutes at the table (or the week on the forum) where the world moves — usually runs like this:

1. Open the downtime view and read the queues first: pending ledger entries, reputation shifts, world-event proposals. Triage them — accept what is true now, dismiss what is not. This single habit prevents ninety percent of downtime confusion.
2. Advance projects: move active ones along, update durations honestly, complete what finished and record how. Start anything new the fiction demands, with a type, an owner, and a duration measured in world time.
3. Tend havens: log what happened to each held place, update threats (a rising threat ignored becomes a critical one on schedule), note new residents or benefits.
4. Record money: log the party's earnings and spending as ledger entries with real titles ("Fencing the Gray Man's silver", not "misc +200"). Debts go down as debts, not as negative income — the books distinguish them for a reason.
5. If time passed in fiction, let the scheduled effects fire with the advance rather than hand-computing upkeep; review what they proposed in the queues.

A concrete example ties it together. The party leaves Winterfort for two months. Before they go, the GM creates a project: repair the north wall, active, owned by the steward, duration measured accordingly. During the absence, weekly upkeep fires upkeep suggestions, a reputation suggestion arrives (the party's standing with the merchants rises after the grain donation), and the haven log gains entries. On return, the GM triages the queues, completes the wall (or extends it — sieges happen), accepts the reputation shift, and the players find a changed home instead of a paused one.

## Campaign configuration

Downtime management is a GM and Co-DM surface by default: creating and editing havens, projects, ledger settings, and scheduled effects, and resolving every suggestion queue. Players participate at the edges — contributing ledger entries, and whatever visibility the campaign grants them over haven and project pages. Whether players see full downtime detail or a redacted view follows the same visibility and discovery rules as the wiki, since havens and projects live there as pages. Currency naming, starting funds, and whether the treasury is shared are ledger settings the GM sets once and rarely revisits.

## Players

Downtime is partly yours to read and partly yours to write. You can log earnings and spending, watch your faction standings move, and follow the haven log like a newspaper from home. You cannot rewrite the books, resolve queues, or edit projects beyond what your campaign allows — those are the GM's levers, and the queues in particular stay trustworthy because only a few hands touch them. If the treasury looks wrong, say so with the entry in front of you; "the books say X, I remember Y" resolves faster than either side guessing.

## Things to know

Suggestions are proposals, not facts — nothing is true until accepted, and a queue left untriaged is a growing pile of maybe. Status progressions are enforced, so plan projects with real middles; the system will not let you skip them, which protects the fiction's pacing. Terminal states are terminal: a completed or failed project rejects further simulation edits, so be sure before closing. Debts are not negative income in the ledger's math — they are tracked apart and summarized separately, which keeps the treasury honest. And downtime pages are wiki pages: deleting a haven page deletes the haven, so check what links to it first, exactly as with any lore page.
